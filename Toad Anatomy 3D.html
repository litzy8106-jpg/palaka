The big picture

Where the manual and the specimen differ
  • The manual's text and atlas describe the frog; your specimen is the cane toad, Rhinella marina (Bufonidae). The plan is the same, but toads have parotoid ("poison") glands, drier warty skin, shorter legs and no teeth.
  • The manual says male toads have vocal sacs. Most references describe a single subgular vocal sac in R. marina males. Go with your instructor's wording on the practical.
  • The checklist lists "atrium" (singular), but the heart has two atria, right and left. The model shows both.
  • Gracilis minor appears in both the ventral and dorsal thigh lists because it wraps the posterior edge of the thigh. It is one muscle.
  • The rectus femoris anticus is listed under the dorsal thigh in the manual; some atlases call it part of the triceps femoris on the anterior-dorsal side.

A · Regions, directions, axes and planes

Directions

What: a fixed vocabulary for "where" that does not change when the specimen is flipped. How: every term is relative to the animal, never to you, so the toad's left stays its left even when it lies ventral side up in the pan. Why: during a move-type practical the specimen may face any way, so "dorsal" and "anterior" are the only descriptions that stay true.

Axes and planes

The antero-posterior (longitudinal) axis runs snout to vent; a dorso-ventral axis crosses it from back to belly. Turn on "Body planes" in Explore to see the three planes below.

  • Median plane: vertical, head to tail, splits the body into equal right and left halves. A sagittal plane is the median plane or any plane parallel to it.
  • Horizontal (frontal) plane: at right angles to the median and transverse planes; splits dorsal from ventral.
  • Transverse plane (cross section): at right angles to the median plane; splits cranial from caudal.
Check yourself

The toad lies ventral side up. Is its heart "above" or "below" its vertebral column in anatomical terms? Ventral to it, regardless of how the pan is turned.

Which plane would cut both eyes in half at once? A transverse or a horizontal plane through the eyes; a median plane would miss both.

Procedure · double pithing and opening the toad

Why the steps go in this order

What: pithing destroys the brain (cranial) and then the spinal cord (spinal) so the animal feels nothing and lies still, while the heart keeps beating for a while. How: the needle enters the crease between the skull and the atlas at 90°, is tipped forward through the foramen magnum about 8–10 mm into the braincase and swept left-right, then pulled back to 1–2 mm, turned to point caudally, and pushed about 30 mm down the neural canal. The legs extend and stiffen as the cord is destroyed, then go flaccid.

Why it matters for anatomy: the needle path is a skeletal lesson in itself. You aim for the crease because the cranium ends and the atlas begins there, and the foramen magnum is the doorway from the neural canal into the braincase.

Opening the body: on a preserved toad you cut a few millimetres right of centre; on a live (pithed) toad you cut along the left external oblique and angle to the chest. Both avoid the anterior abdominal vein, which runs along the midventral wall to the liver. Then the pectoral girdle is cut and spread to expose the heart.

Skinning a leg: preserved toads are skinned by cutting the ventral skin and unwrapping; freshly pithed toads peel like a stocking from the thigh downward, worked along the joints.
